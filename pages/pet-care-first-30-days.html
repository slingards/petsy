Pet Care Checklist: Your First 30 Days With a New Pet

A practical 30-day checklist for new pet owners: what to prepare, what to do in the first week, when to see a veterinarian, and which warning signs need urgent care.

Animal Care Questions Help Center
Home / Animal Care Questions / Pet Care Checklist: Your First 30 Days With a New Pet

Before your pet comes home

Preparation prevents most first-week problems. Research the species and breed so you understand adult size, lifespan, diet, and legal requirements, and book a veterinarian who treats your type of animal before you need one.

  • Set up a quiet, secure area with the correct enclosure, bedding, food and water dishes, and species-appropriate heating or lighting if needed.
  • Pet-proof the home: store medicines and cleaners, secure cords, and remove toxic plants (see Environment and Behaviour).
  • Buy the same food the animal is currently eating so you can change diets gradually if you choose to.
  • Ask for health records, vaccination and parasite treatment history, and any identification or legal documents.

Days 1 to 3: settle in gently

New animals are often stressed. Keep routines predictable, limit visitors, and let the animal approach you instead of forcing contact. Offer food and clean water, and watch eating, drinking, droppings, and energy. Our answer on helping a new pet settle into your home covers this in more detail.

Week 1: veterinary visit and routines

Arrange a first veterinary examination within the first days, or as advised by the seller or shelter and local rules. Ask about vaccination, parasite prevention, identification such as a microchip, sterilization, diet, and species-specific housing checks. Start simple routines for feeding, cleaning, exercise, and rest.

Weeks 2 to 4: training, enrichment, and monitoring

Begin gentle, reward-based training and add enrichment that suits the species, such as puzzle feeders, climbing areas, hides, or safe chewing items. Weigh or check body condition regularly, keep a simple health log, and introduce other pets slowly (see how to introduce two pets safely).

Warning signs that need a veterinarian

Do not wait if you see trouble breathing, collapse, repeated vomiting, a rabbit or small mammal that stops eating, inability to urinate, seizures, severe bleeding, or suspected poisoning. For anything unusual or persistent, contact a veterinarian promptly.

Budget for the whole life of the animal

Plan for food, routine and emergency veterinary care, housing, parasite prevention, grooming, training, insurance where available, and travel or boarding. Costs vary by species and country, and long-lived animals need a long-term commitment.
